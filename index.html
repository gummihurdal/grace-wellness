The idea and the market

Menopause care in Europe is thin. A woman gets a short GP appointment and a yes or no on hormone therapy. If she wants something gentler, or alongside, she's left to a supermarket shelf and an internet full of contradictory advice. Meanwhile the US market has shown that brand + doctor + program is what people will pay for, and pay for monthly.

Grace puts those three together for the German-speaking market first: a small, coherent product line, a named physician who explains how each product works and when it won't, and a membership built around eight pillars of daily living — because a magnesium capsule does very little for someone who drinks wine at 10 p.m. and sleeps in a warm room.

The product is the entry

Every bottle includes the free tier of the program. The bottle is the membership card.

The doctor is the moat

Dr. Alina answers questions, teaches one pillar a week live, and is bookable 1:1. Nobody copies that by copying an ingredient list.

Europe-native from day one

EU food-supplement rules, EU wording, EU manufacturing, German-language content. Not a US brand translated.

The program

An always-on membership built on eight pillars — nutrition, exercise, water, sunlight, temperance, air, rest, trust — written for midlife specifically, led by a doctor.

Pillar library

Short lessons per pillar. Not generic wellness: "temperance at 52" is about alcohol and caffeine timing and night sweats, not abstract virtue.

Dr. Alina live, weekly

One open session a week. Twenty minutes on a pillar, then questions. Recorded — the library builds itself. One hour of her time, hundreds of members.

Ask the doctor

Written questions answered in batches. Scales far better than 1:1 and the answers become content.

1:1 telehealth

Bookable video consultations. Pay per session, or included monthly at the top tier. This is the premium layer and the one that needs the most legal care.

Daily practice

A small daily prompt tied to the pillar you're working on. Push notification, two minutes.

The circle

A members' community. Women in this transition are often isolated in it; this is where retention lives.

Business model

Revenue lines

Supplements at €39–49 per bottle, 20% off on subscription. Landed cost around €7–10 per unit at small volume, so roughly 75–80% gross margin before marketing.

Membership at €19 and €99. Near-zero marginal cost on the €19 tier; the €99 tier is capped by the doctor's hours.

Consultations at €90–140 per 30 minutes, revenue-shared with the physician.

What the numbers need to look like

A member on two bottles plus the €19 tier is about €95 a month in revenue. At 300 such members the business is roughly €340k a year and can pay a part-time doctor, a part-time facilitator and the founders something.

Direct-to-consumer acquisition in supplements is expensive — expect €40–80 to acquire a customer. The program is what justifies that: it turns a €45 one-off sale into a multi-month relationship.

Go-to-market

Switzerland and Germany first, German and English. Channels, in order of expected return: Dr. Alina's own practice and referral network; midlife women's communities and podcasts; search content around specific symptoms; paid social only once organic conversion is understood. Pharmacies and practitioners as a second phase — Switzerland in particular sells wellness through the Apotheke.

Time to market

Nine to twelve months to first sale on the lean path. The long pole is manufacturing lead time, not the website.

  • Month 0–1
    Decide positioning with Grace and Dr. Alina. Name and trademark search. Company formed. Physician agreement drafted, including disclosure of commercial interest.
  • Month 1–3
    Formulation with a partner. Dose and interaction review by the physician. Regulatory check of every ingredient against EU and Swiss lists (black cohosh, vitex and ashwagandha each have country-specific limits).
  • Month 3–4
    Manufacturer selected, samples approved, label artwork finalised, notifications filed. Program content outlined; first weekly sessions run as a free pilot with a dozen women.
  • Month 4–8
    Production run (typical lead time 10–16 weeks). Lab testing. Platform built: shop, membership, scheduling, video. Pillar library written and recorded.
  • Month 8–10
    Soft launch to the pilot group and Dr. Alina's network. Fix what breaks. Collect the first testimonials you're allowed to use.
  • Month 10–12
    Public launch in Switzerland. Germany follows once the notification and shipping are in place. Decide on SKUs three and four from real demand.

Chance of success

Scored honestly, 0–10, where "success" means a business that pays its people and still exists in three years. Averages hide the story, so here is each factor.

Market need
Real and growing. Midlife women are underserved and increasingly vocal about it. Menopause is finally a mainstream topic in Europe.
8
Differentiation
The doctor-led program is genuinely rare in Europe. The supplements alone are not — the shelf is full.
7
Founder fit
Strong on platform, security and GDPR — unusual for a supplement founder. Weak on consumer marketing and supply chain; those have to be hired or learned.
6
The physician
The whole concept rests on one person's licensing, time and willingness to put her name on it. Not yet confirmed. Key-person risk is the largest single risk in the plan.
5
Regulatory path
Supplements are manageable. Telehealth across borders is not — licensing is national and conflicts of interest between prescribing and selling are scrutinised.
5
Unit economics
Margins are good; acquisition cost is the killer. Most D2C supplement brands die on marketing spend. The membership improves this but doesn't remove it.
4
Capital & time
€75k and a year, run alongside a demanding full-time job and several other projects. Focus is the scarcest input here, scarcer than money.

As a supplement brand alone this is a 3. The program and the doctor lift it to a 5, and a confirmed physician plus a successful 12-person pilot would lift it to a 6 or 7 before any money is spent on inventory.
